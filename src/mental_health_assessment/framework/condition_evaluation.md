# Condition Evaluation Framework 

## Purpose

The Condition Evaluation Framework provides a structured, evidence-based methodology for evaluating higher-level psychological conditions from the behavioural assessment generated by Behavioural Assessment v1 or Behavioural Assessment v2.

These conditions are **behavioural interpretations**, not clinical diagnoses. They represent recurring psychological patterns that are commonly associated with specific combinations of Mental Health Parameter changes, behavioural themes, longitudinal progression and contextual evidence.

The framework serves as a reference for both Behavioural Assessment (v1/v2) and Condition Evaluation.

* Behavioural Assessment may consult this framework when behavioural evidence strongly aligns with one of the predefined conditions.
* Condition Evaluation uses this framework to perform a complete evaluation of all relevant conditions.

The predefined conditions are **reference templates**, not an exhaustive taxonomy. Behavioural Assessment remains free to generate new behavioural themes or condition-like interpretations when the observed behavioural patterns are not adequately represented by the predefined set.

---

# Framework-wide Guidelines

## 1. Evidence Threshold

This section defines when the framework should be meaningfully consulted.

General principles:

* A condition should never be inferred from a single anomalous parameter.
* Core behavioural changes should be supported by related parameter changes, behavioural themes and contextual evidence.
* Longitudinal consistency strengthens interpretation.
* Strong protective factors should moderate the interpretation.
* Contradictory evidence should reduce confidence.
* If behavioural evidence does not sufficiently support a predefined condition, Behavioural Assessment should generate an original behavioural interpretation instead of forcing a match.

Examples and specific thresholds can be added later as we refine the framework.

---

## 2. Interpretation & Language Guidelines

These guidelines apply to every condition.

The framework should:

* describe behavioural patterns rather than diagnose disorders;
* use cautious, evidence-based language;
* explain *why* a condition is being considered;
* acknowledge conflicting or protective evidence;
* allow multiple conditions to coexist when supported;
* avoid implying certainty where evidence is limited.

Behavioural Assessment should reference a predefined condition only when it provides meaningful explanatory value.

Otherwise, it should produce its own evidence-based behavioural interpretation.

Each parameter should indicate whether

* increasing values strengthen the interpretation, or
* decreasing values strengthen the interpretation.

## 3. Parameter Relationships

This section defines interactions that cannot be captured by considering parameters independently.

Examples include:

* combinations of Core Indicators;
* reinforcing parameter pairs;
* protective interactions;
* contradictory evidence;
* long-term versus immediate parameter interactions.

This is where much of the framework's behavioural reasoning will live.

---

## 4. Behavioural Evidence

This describes the behavioural themes and behavioural progression commonly associated with the condition.

These are not requirements, but recurring behavioural patterns that strengthen the interpretation.

Examples:

* persistent mental strain;
* declining engagement;
* worsening social behaviour;
* increasing emotional instability.

---

## 5. Caveats

Defines situations where the condition should not be inferred.

Examples include:

* isolated parameter changes;
* short-lived behavioural fluctuations;
* stronger explanations provided by another condition;
* insufficient behavioural evidence;
* conflicting protective factors.

This section prevents overinterpretation.

---

## 6. Protective Factors

Lists behavioural characteristics that weaken or moderate the interpretation.

Protective factors are evaluated separately from supporting evidence.

Examples include:

* improving resilience;
* healthy coping ability;
* increasing optimism;
* improving social support;
* stable behavioural functioning despite isolated anomalies.

Framework-wide role definitions

These should appear once in the general Condition Evaluation section rather than being redefined under every condition.

Core Indicator: Central evidence for the condition. The condition ordinarily cannot be justified without an appropriate pattern in this parameter.
Reinforcing Indicator: Does not independently establish the condition but substantially strengthens it when it changes alongside a Core Indicator.
Supporting Indicator: Relevant evidence similar to a Core Indicator, but less essential and less discriminative. It supports the interpretation without being required.
Consequence: A parameter change that may develop because the condition has persisted. It helps establish the condition’s behavioural impact rather than its initial presence.
Protective Factor: A capacity or resource that can reduce, moderate or help the user recover from the condition. Weakening of the factor may support concern; strength or improvement may weaken it.
Contextual Factor: Adds useful emotional or behavioural context but should not significantly determine the interpretation by itself.

Supporting Indicators are lesser Core Indicators. Reinforcing Indicators become especially meaningful through their interaction with Core Indicators.

Importance remains separate:

Primary: Crucial to evaluating the condition.
Secondary: Meaningfully contributes but is not indispensable.
Tertiary: Provides additional corroboration or indicates a possible consequence.

Direction means the change that supports the condition interpretation:

↑: An increase supports the condition.
↓: A decrease supports the condition.

# Chronic Stress

## 1. Purpose

Chronic Stress represents a prolonged state of sustained psychological pressure in which stress-related behavioural patterns persist over time and begin influencing emotional wellbeing, cognitive functioning, daily behaviour, recovery and overall resilience.

Within this framework, Chronic Stress is interpreted as a behavioural pattern rather than a clinical diagnosis. Rather than representing temporary reactions to isolated stressors, it reflects persistent difficulty recovering from ongoing demands. The evaluation should therefore distinguish enduring patterns of strain from expected short-term responses to challenging situations, recognising that prolonged exposure to stress may gradually influence multiple aspects of psychological functioning.

### Review of the assignments

**Stress** is the Primary Core Indicator because it represents the defining feature of the condition: persistent psychological pressure. **Anxiety** and **Rumination** are Primary Reinforcing Indicators, as they reflect sustained anticipation of stressors and difficulty mentally disengaging from them. **Coping Ability**, **Resilience** and **Self-efficacy** are Primary Protective Factors because they strongly influence whether elevated Stress becomes entrenched or whether the user retains the capacity to manage and recover from continuing demands.

**Sleep Quality** and **Emotional Exhaustion** are Secondary Consequences because they commonly emerge after prolonged strain begins affecting recovery and emotional resources. **Sentiment Score** provides Secondary Supporting evidence of a broader negative shift in emotional tone, while **Frustration** acts as a Secondary Reinforcing Indicator when recurring demands, obstacles, or perceived lack of control intensify an existing stress pattern. **Optimism** and **Social Support** are Secondary Protective Factors that influence the user’s perception of difficulty and access to recovery resources. **Mental Fatigue**, **Concentration**, **Motivation** and **Task Engagement** remain Tertiary Consequences, while **Self-talk Score** and **Sadness** provide additional context without independently determining the condition.

---

## 3. Parameter Relationships

The following examples illustrate how the model should reason about Chronic Stress rather than serving as rigid decision rules.

* **Persistently increasing Stress accompanied by increasing Anxiety and Rumination** provides substantially stronger evidence for Chronic Stress than elevated Stress alone. Together, they indicate both continued pressure and difficulty psychologically disengaging from its causes.

* **Rising Stress accompanied by increasing Frustration** suggests that recurring obstacles, excessive demands or reduced perceived control are intensifying the user’s strain. Frustration should reinforce the interpretation only when a sustained Stress pattern is already present.

* **Increasing Frustration without persistent Stress** should not substantially support Chronic Stress. It may instead reflect a temporary setback, interpersonal conflict or dissatisfaction with a specific situation.

* **Rising Stress with declining Coping Ability, Resilience or Self-efficacy** suggests that ongoing demands are increasingly exceeding the user’s perceived or actual capacity to manage them, making prolonged stress considerably more likely.

* **High Stress while Coping Ability, Resilience and Self-efficacy remain strong** should not immediately produce the highest level of concern. The user may be experiencing considerable pressure while retaining effective recovery mechanisms. This pattern should continue to be monitored, particularly if Stress persists or the protective factors begin declining.

* **Persistent Stress followed by declining Sleep Quality and increasing Emotional Exhaustion** suggests that prolonged strain is beginning to interfere with recovery and emotional wellbeing rather than remaining a temporary stress response.

* **Declining Sentiment Score alongside persistent Stress, Anxiety or Frustration** strengthens the interpretation that sustained pressure is affecting the user’s broader emotional state rather than remaining confined to a particular situation.

* **Mental Fatigue, reduced Concentration, declining Motivation and reduced Task Engagement** should primarily be interpreted as downstream consequences. They meaningfully strengthen the interpretation only when the central stress pattern has already been established.

* **Strong Social Support alongside elevated Stress** should moderate concern by suggesting that the user retains meaningful external resources that may reduce perceived burden and support recovery.

* **Improving Coping Ability, Resilience or Self-efficacy despite elevated Stress** may indicate adaptation rather than deterioration. The framework should recognise that high Stress alone does not necessarily imply worsening Chronic Stress when protective capacities remain stable or improve.

* **Stress and Frustration beginning to decline while Sleep Quality, Sentiment and Coping Ability improve** suggests recovery, even if consequences such as Emotional Exhaustion or Mental Fatigue remain temporarily elevated.

* **High Stress without persistence, reinforcing patterns or weakening protective factors** may represent temporary situational pressure rather than Chronic Stress.

---

## 4. Behavioural Evidence

Behavioural themes that may strengthen the interpretation include:

* persistent psychological pressure;
* sustained mental strain across multiple days or weeks;
* difficulty mentally disengaging from ongoing responsibilities or concerns;
* repeated anticipation of future stressors;
* recurring frustration with demands, obstacles or a perceived lack of control;
* increasing emotional burden despite continued functioning;
* gradually declining ability to recover between demanding periods;
* growing perception that everyday demands are becoming more difficult to manage;
* sustained functioning that requires progressively greater mental effort;
* reduced adaptability to continuing pressures;
* recurring worry that remains present even during periods intended for rest;
* gradually accumulating strain rather than isolated episodes of stress;
* persistent feelings of being under pressure despite temporary improvements in workload.

These themes are examples rather than required labels. Behavioural Assessment v1 or v2 may generate different behavioural descriptions that capture the same underlying pattern.

---

## 5. Caveats

Chronic Stress should not be inferred merely because:

* Stress temporarily increases following an isolated difficult event;
* Elevated Stress occurs during an expected short-term demanding period;
* Anxiety, Rumination or Frustration temporarily increase without persistent Stress;
* Frustration is tied to a single obstacle or conflict and resolves once the situation changes;
* Sleep Quality or Emotional Exhaustion changes in isolation without evidence of prolonged psychological strain;
* Mental Fatigue or reduced Concentration appears during examinations, deadlines or temporary workload increases but improves with recovery;
* strong Protective Factors remain stable, and the user continues recovering effectively;
* Stress is already declining while remaining negative parameters appear to be delayed consequences of an earlier stressful period;
* the behavioural pattern is better explained by Burnout, Emotional Dysregulation or another condition;
* there is insufficient evidence that the pressure has persisted beyond a temporary response.

The model should distinguish **persistent psychological strain from temporary pressure**. High Stress alone does not imply Chronic Stress; persistence, reinforcing evidence and reduced recovery are central to the interpretation.

The model should also recognise that **Chronic Stress and Burnout are related but distinct patterns**. Chronic Stress centres on sustained psychological pressure and impaired recovery, whereas Burnout additionally involves meaningful depletion, disengagement and reduced perceived effectiveness.

---

## 6. Protective Factors

Protective factors do not eliminate the possibility of Chronic Stress. Instead, they indicate whether the user retains sufficient psychological resources to manage continuing demands and recover from their effects.

* **Strong Coping Ability** suggests that the user continues managing stress through effective behavioural or cognitive strategies. Elevated Stress and Frustration should still be monitored, but concern is moderated when coping consistently supports genuine recovery rather than merely maintaining performance.

* **High or improving Resilience** indicates that periods of stress are followed by meaningful recovery rather than progressively accumulating strain. Persistent Stress accompanied by stable Resilience should therefore be interpreted more cautiously.

* **Stable Self-efficacy** suggests that the user continues believing they can successfully manage current challenges. This weakens the interpretation that ongoing demands are overwhelming their psychological resources, even when Stress remains elevated.

* **Stable or improving Optimism** indicates that the user continues expecting present difficulties to improve or remain manageable. While it does not negate sustained Stress, it reduces the likelihood that the pattern will become increasingly entrenched.

* **Healthy Social Support** may reduce perceived burden, provide practical or emotional assistance and help the user interpret difficulties as more manageable. Strong support should meaningfully moderate concern, particularly when accompanied by stable Coping Ability and Resilience.

* **High Coping Ability alongside increasing Stress or Frustration** should not be treated as the utmost cause for concern because the user may reasonably be expected to adapt and recover. The pattern should nevertheless be monitored to ensure that coping remains effective and does not begin declining under continued pressure.

* **Behavioural evidence of consistent recovery**, such as improving Coping Ability, Resilience, Self-efficacy, Sentiment or Sleep Quality after stressful periods, should substantially weaken the interpretation of an entrenched Chronic Stress pattern.

* **Continued exposure to stress alone should not outweigh strong protective evidence.** A user may experience prolonged demands while consistently demonstrating effective coping, resilience and recovery. The framework should recognise sustained adaptation rather than assuming that persistent external pressure necessarily produces Chronic Stress.

# Burnout

## 1. Purpose

Burnout represents a prolonged pattern of depletion that develops when sustained demands repeatedly consume the user's mental and emotional resources without sufficient recovery. It is reflected not merely by feeling pressured, but by an interaction between exhaustion, declining engagement, reduced motivation and a growing sense of ineffectiveness toward the activities creating the strain.

Within this framework, Burnout is treated as a behavioural interpretation rather than a clinical diagnosis. Although formal definitions generally restrict burnout to occupational settings, this project applies the pattern operationally to sustained academic, professional or similarly demanding goal-directed activity. The evaluation should therefore identify the relevant domain rather than treating general tiredness or distress as burnout.

### Review of the assignments

**Emotional Exhaustion** and **Mental Fatigue** are the primary Core Indicators because they capture the defining feature of Burnout: sustained depletion of emotional and cognitive resources. **Task Engagement** is also Primary, as reduced engagement is one of the most characteristic behavioural manifestations of Burnout, although it should reinforce rather than independently establish the condition. **Motivation** is treated as a Primary Consequence because prolonged depletion commonly results in reduced willingness to continue meaningful work, even though low motivation alone is insufficient evidence.

**Stress** remains a Secondary Reinforcing Indicator because Burnout generally develops from prolonged stress, but persistent stress without depletion is better interpreted as Chronic Stress. **Self-efficacy**, **Concentration** and **Sentiment Score** provide important supporting evidence, reflecting reduced effectiveness, impaired cognitive functioning and a broader negative emotional tone. **Rumination** remains a Tertiary Reinforcing Indicator, as continued mental engagement with work or responsibilities may prolong recovery but is not central to every burnout presentation. **Physical Fatigue** is retained only as a Tertiary Consequence because it is relatively non-specific, while **Coping Ability**, **Resilience**, **Optimism** and **Social Support** serve as Protective Factors that moderate concern by indicating the user's capacity to recover despite prolonged demands.

---

# 3. Parameter Relationships

The following examples illustrate how the model should reason about Burnout rather than serving as rigid decision rules.

* **Persistent Emotional Exhaustion and Mental Fatigue accompanied by declining Task Engagement** provide the strongest overall evidence for Burnout. Together they indicate both resource depletion and psychological withdrawal from the demanding activity.

* **Exhaustion combined with declining Self-efficacy** suggests that the user is beginning to feel ineffective despite continued effort, strengthening the reduced-effectiveness aspect of Burnout.

* **Persistent Stress followed by increasing Emotional Exhaustion and Mental Fatigue** may indicate progression from Chronic Stress into Burnout. Stress provides the prolonged demand, while exhaustion reflects depletion of the user's internal resources.

* **High Stress without meaningful Emotional Exhaustion or declining Task Engagement** should generally be interpreted as Chronic Stress rather than Burnout.

* **High Emotional Exhaustion with preserved Task Engagement, Motivation and Self-efficacy** may represent temporary overextension rather than Burnout. The user appears depleted but remains psychologically invested in the activity. This pattern should be monitored for progression rather than immediately interpreted as Burnout.

* **Declining Motivation without exhaustion** should not be interpreted as Burnout. It may instead reflect boredom, changing priorities, dissatisfaction or reduced interest.

* **Reduced Task Engagement with relatively stable Mental Fatigue and Emotional Exhaustion** may indicate ordinary disengagement or dissatisfaction rather than Burnout.

* **Reduced Concentration** strengthens the interpretation primarily when it develops alongside sustained exhaustion, rather than in isolation.

* **Persistent Rumination about work, responsibilities or unfinished tasks** may reinforce Burnout by reducing opportunities for psychological recovery, but should not substantially influence the interpretation unless the central depletion pattern is already present.

* **Strong Coping Ability, Resilience and Social Support** should moderate concern. Even when exhaustion is increasing, these protective factors suggest that recovery remains possible if demands reduce or coping remains effective.

* **Improving Motivation, Task Engagement and Self-efficacy despite temporarily elevated exhaustion** suggests recovery. Behavioural reinvestment often improves before fatigue completely resolves.

* **Burnout may coexist with Chronic Stress**, but the two should not be treated interchangeably. Chronic Stress primarily reflects persistent psychological pressure, whereas Burnout additionally requires sustained depletion, reduced engagement and declining effectiveness.

---

# 4. Behavioural Evidence

Behavioural themes that may strengthen the interpretation include:

* sustained emotional and mental depletion;
* progressively declining engagement in academic, professional or other demanding activities;
* continuing responsibilities mechanically rather than with genuine involvement;
* feeling emotionally detached from previously meaningful work;
* reduced willingness to initiate or continue demanding tasks;
* increasing perception that continued effort produces little meaningful progress;
* gradually declining confidence in personal effectiveness;
* reduced cognitive endurance during sustained work;
* increasing avoidance of demanding responsibilities;
* functioning primarily through obligation rather than interest or purpose;
* inadequate recovery despite periods of rest;
* feeling "drained" even before beginning familiar responsibilities.

These themes are examples rather than required labels. Behavioural Assessment v1 or v2 may generate different behavioural descriptions that capture the same underlying pattern.

---

# 5. Caveats

Burnout should not be inferred merely because:

* the user feels tired following an unusually demanding day or week;
* Stress is elevated without persistent emotional or cognitive exhaustion;
* Mental Fatigue improves rapidly after adequate rest;
* Motivation declines because the user simply loses interest in a specific activity;
* Task Engagement temporarily falls following disappointment or failure;
* Concentration decreases during short-lived examination periods or deadlines;
* Physical Fatigue occurs without meaningful emotional or cognitive depletion;
* Self-efficacy temporarily decreases following an isolated setback before recovering;
* the behavioural pattern is better explained by Chronic Stress, Depression or temporary overwork;
* there is no identifiable domain involving prolonged and sustained demands.

The model should distinguish **Burnout from ordinary fatigue**. Temporary tiredness usually improves after sufficient recovery, whereas Burnout reflects a broader pattern of persistent depletion, disengagement and reduced effectiveness.

The model should also distinguish **Burnout from Depression**. Burnout generally remains centred around a sustained domain of demand (such as academics or work), whereas depressive patterns are typically broader and extend across multiple areas of life. Although the two may coexist, evidence supporting one should not automatically imply the other.

---

# 6. Protective Factors

Protective factors do not eliminate the possibility of Burnout. Instead, they help determine whether the user retains sufficient psychological resources to recover from sustained demands.

* **Strong Coping Ability** suggests that the user continues to manage prolonged demands effectively. Increasing exhaustion should still be monitored, but concern is moderated when coping consistently results in meaningful recovery rather than simply allowing continued functioning.

* **High or improving Resilience** indicates that demanding periods are followed by recovery instead of accumulating depletion. Persistent exhaustion accompanied by stable resilience should therefore be interpreted more cautiously.

* **Stable Self-efficacy** suggests that the user continues believing they can successfully meet ongoing demands. This weakens the reduced-effectiveness component of Burnout, even if temporary exhaustion is present.

* **Stable or improving Optimism** indicates that the user continues to view current demands as manageable or worthwhile. While it does not negate evidence of Burnout, it reduces the likelihood that depletion will become progressively entrenched.

* **Healthy Social Support** provides practical and emotional resources that may reduce burden, encourage recovery and help the user establish healthier boundaries. Its presence should meaningfully moderate concern, particularly when accompanied by improving engagement and wellbeing.

* **Behavioural evidence of genuine recovery**—such as improving Motivation, Task Engagement or Self-efficacy following reduced demands—should substantially weaken the interpretation of Burnout, even if Mental Fatigue temporarily remains elevated.

* **Continued performance alone should not be interpreted as a Protective Factor.** A user may continue functioning despite progressively worsening exhaustion. The framework should recognise recovery and adaptation, not merely endurance.

# Cognitive Disengagement

## 1. Purpose

Cognitive Disengagement represents a behavioural pattern in which the user progressively withdraws mental effort, attention and psychological investment from meaningful activities or responsibilities. Rather than reflecting an inability to perform, it reflects a reduced willingness or tendency to cognitively engage with tasks, resulting in diminished focus, persistence and involvement.

Within this framework, Cognitive Disengagement is interpreted as a behavioural pattern rather than a clinical diagnosis. It may develop following prolonged stress, repeated setbacks, reduced interest or other psychological difficulties, but it may also occur independently. The evaluation should therefore distinguish genuine cognitive withdrawal from temporary distraction, fatigue or situational loss of interest, recognising that disengagement reflects a persistent reduction in mental investment rather than simply reduced productivity.

### Review of the assignments

**Task Engagement** is the Primary Core Indicator because Cognitive Disengagement is fundamentally characterised by reduced psychological involvement in meaningful activities. **Mental Fatigue** is a Primary Reinforcing Indicator, as sustained cognitive exhaustion often accelerates withdrawal from demanding tasks without independently defining the condition. **Concentration** provides Primary Supporting evidence by reflecting reduced cognitive investment, while **Motivation** serves as a Secondary Core Indicator because diminished willingness to engage commonly accompanies, but does not solely define, disengagement.

**Self-efficacy**, **Sentiment Score** and **Stress** provide Secondary supporting and reinforcing evidence by indicating declining confidence, broader emotional changes and external pressures that may contribute to withdrawal. **Coping Ability** functions as a Secondary Protective Factor because effective coping may prevent temporary disengagement from becoming persistent. **Rumination** remains a Secondary Contextual Factor by providing insight into the user's mental state without directly establishing disengagement. **Emotional Exhaustion**, **Resilience**, **Optimism**, **Social Support** and **Self-talk Score** provide additional reinforcing, protective or contextual information that helps refine the overall interpretation.

---

# 3. Parameter Relationships

The following examples illustrate how the model should reason about Cognitive Disengagement rather than serving as rigid decision rules.

* **Declining Task Engagement accompanied by reduced Concentration** provides the strongest overall evidence for Cognitive Disengagement, indicating reduced mental investment rather than isolated attention difficulties.

* **Increasing Mental Fatigue alongside declining Task Engagement** strengthens the interpretation by suggesting that sustained cognitive effort is becoming increasingly difficult to maintain.

* **Reduced Motivation accompanying declining Task Engagement** suggests broader withdrawal from meaningful activities rather than temporary distraction or loss of focus.

* **Declining Self-efficacy together with reduced Engagement** may indicate that repeated setbacks or perceived ineffectiveness are contributing to cognitive withdrawal.

* **Persistent Stress followed by declining Engagement** may represent progression from sustained pressure toward behavioural disengagement, particularly when cognitive investment steadily decreases.

* **Poor Concentration without reduced Task Engagement** should not substantially support Cognitive Disengagement, as concentration difficulties may arise from fatigue, distraction or situational factors.

* **High Motivation despite temporarily reduced Engagement** may indicate short-term interruption rather than persistent disengagement and should be monitored before stronger conclusions are drawn.

* **Increasing Rumination** may provide useful context by suggesting that cognitive resources remain occupied by unresolved concerns, but it should not independently strengthen the interpretation.

* **Strong Coping Ability despite elevated Stress** should moderate concern by suggesting that temporary disengagement is less likely to become persistent.

* **Improving Engagement, Motivation and Concentration** suggests recovery, even if Mental Fatigue temporarily remains elevated.

* **Reduced Engagement confined to one specific activity** should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect changing interests or priorities rather than broader Cognitive Disengagement.

* **Cognitive Disengagement may coexist with Burnout or Depression**, but it should remain centred on persistent withdrawal of mental investment rather than emotional depletion or pervasive low mood.

---

# 4. Behavioural Evidence

Behavioural themes that may strengthen the interpretation include:

* progressively reduced involvement in meaningful tasks;
* declining willingness to mentally invest in responsibilities;
* frequent loss of focus during sustained activities;
* completing tasks with minimal psychological involvement;
* increasing avoidance of cognitively demanding work;
* reduced persistence when facing mental challenges;
* feeling mentally detached from ongoing responsibilities;
* difficulty maintaining attention despite intending to engage;
* gradually declining initiative toward previously meaningful activities;
* requiring greater effort to remain mentally engaged;
* repeatedly postponing tasks because sustained engagement feels difficult;
* reduced curiosity or cognitive investment during learning or problem solving;
* persistent withdrawal of mental effort across multiple situations.

These themes are examples rather than required labels. Behavioural Assessment v1 or v2 may generate different behavioural descriptions that capture the same underlying pattern.

---

# 5. Caveats

Cognitive Disengagement should not be inferred merely because:

* Concentration temporarily decreases during periods of fatigue or distraction;
* Motivation declines toward a single activity while remaining stable elsewhere;
* Engagement briefly decreases following disappointment or failure;
* Mental Fatigue improves after adequate recovery;
* Stress temporarily reduces cognitive performance without persistent disengagement;
* the user intentionally disengages from activities that no longer align with personal goals;
* reduced Engagement is fully explained by Burnout or Depression;
* the behavioural pattern reflects ordinary procrastination rather than sustained cognitive withdrawal;
* protective factors remain strong and engagement subsequently improves;
* there is insufficient evidence that disengagement persists across time or situations.

The model should distinguish **persistent cognitive withdrawal from temporary fluctuations in attention or interest**. Reduced engagement should be interpreted as a sustained behavioural pattern rather than an isolated response to workload, boredom or short-term fatigue.

---

# 6. Protective Factors

Protective factors do not eliminate the possibility of Cognitive Disengagement. Instead, they indicate whether the user retains the psychological resources needed to remain mentally invested in meaningful activities.

* **Strong Coping Ability** suggests that temporary disengagement is more likely to resolve before becoming persistent, particularly when effective strategies restore focus and involvement.

* **High Resilience** indicates that setbacks or demanding periods are followed by renewed engagement rather than progressive withdrawal.

* **Stable or improving Optimism** suggests that the user continues perceiving future effort as worthwhile, reducing the likelihood of sustained disengagement.

* **Healthy Social Support** may encourage accountability, provide encouragement and help re-establish engagement with meaningful responsibilities.

* **Improving Task Engagement and Concentration** should substantially weaken the interpretation of Cognitive Disengagement, even if Mental Fatigue temporarily remains elevated.

* **Increasing Self-efficacy** indicates that the user is regaining confidence in their ability to engage successfully with demanding tasks.

* **Behavioural evidence of renewed curiosity, initiative or persistence** suggests recovery of cognitive investment rather than continued disengagement.

* **Temporary reductions in engagement should not outweigh consistent evidence of recovery.** The framework should recognise renewed psychological involvement rather than assuming that isolated lapses represent persistent Cognitive Disengagement.

# Emotional Dysregulation

## 1. Purpose

Emotional Dysregulation represents a behavioural pattern in which the user experiences persistent difficulty managing, modulating or recovering from emotional responses. Rather than reflecting the presence of negative emotions alone, it describes a reduced ability to regulate emotional reactions in a manner that is proportionate to the situation, resulting in emotions that become difficult to control, sustain or recover from.

Within this framework, Emotional Dysregulation is interpreted as a behavioural pattern rather than a clinical diagnosis. The evaluation should therefore distinguish temporary emotional reactions from persistent patterns of impaired emotional regulation. Experiencing anxiety, frustration or distress during genuinely difficult situations is a normal psychological response and should not, by itself, support this condition.

Instead, the framework focuses on the relationship between emotional experiences and the user's ability to recover from them. Emotional Dysregulation becomes more plausible when emotions appear to persist beyond their immediate triggers, become increasingly difficult to regulate, or repeatedly interfere with cognitive functioning, behaviour or daily responsibilities. The emphasis is therefore placed on emotional control and recovery rather than emotional intensity alone.

### Review of the assignments

**Frustration** and **Anxiety** are the Primary Core Indicators because they best capture the emotional experiences around which dysregulation commonly develops. Neither parameter independently establishes Emotional Dysregulation, but together they provide the strongest behavioural foundation when accompanied by evidence that emotions persist or become difficult to regulate. **Stress** and **Rumination** function as Secondary Reinforcing Indicators because prolonged psychological pressure and repetitive emotional processing commonly sustain dysregulated emotional states without defining them directly.

**Sentiment Score** and **Self-talk Score** provide Secondary Supporting evidence by reflecting broader changes in emotional tone and internal emotional processing. **Emotional Exhaustion** is treated as a Secondary Consequence because prolonged emotional dysregulation may gradually deplete emotional resources. **Coping Ability** and **Resilience** serve as Secondary Protective Factors by influencing the user's capacity to regulate emotional experiences and recover from them. **Concentration**, **Motivation**, **Optimism** and **Social Support** provide additional consequence or protective evidence that helps refine the interpretation without forming its behavioural core.

---

# 3. Parameter Relationships

The following examples illustrate how the model should reason about Emotional Dysregulation rather than serving as rigid decision rules.

* **Persistent Anxiety accompanied by increasing Frustration** provides the strongest overall evidence for Emotional Dysregulation, particularly when emotions appear increasingly difficult to regulate or recover from.

* **Stress followed by sustained Anxiety and Frustration** suggests that external demands may be driving impaired emotional regulation rather than temporary emotional reactions.

* **Increasing Rumination alongside Anxiety or Frustration** strengthens the interpretation by suggesting that emotional responses continue beyond the original trigger, reducing opportunities for emotional recovery.

* **Negative Sentiment Score accompanied by increasingly negative Self-talk** supports the interpretation that emotional difficulties are becoming more pervasive rather than remaining situational.

* **Emotional Exhaustion developing after prolonged emotional difficulty** suggests that repeated emotional regulation efforts are becoming increasingly demanding.

* **High Anxiety that remains proportional to an objectively stressful situation** should not substantially support Emotional Dysregulation unless there is additional evidence of impaired regulation or recovery.

* **Temporary Frustration following an isolated setback** should not independently support this condition.

* **Strong Coping Ability and Resilience despite elevated emotional responses** should moderate concern, as the user may still regulate emotions effectively and recover appropriately.

* **Improving Coping Ability together with declining Anxiety and Frustration** suggests recovery even if temporary Emotional Exhaustion remains.

* **Emotional Dysregulation may coexist with Chronic Stress, Burnout or Depression**, but it should remain centred on persistent difficulty regulating emotional responses rather than sustained pressure, depletion or low mood alone.

* **Persistent emotional responses across multiple situations** strengthen the interpretation more than emotional reactions confined to a single event.

* **Improving emotional recovery despite continued stressors** should weaken the interpretation because the central difficulty lies in regulation rather than exposure to negative experiences.

---

# 4. Behavioural Evidence

Behavioural themes that may strengthen the interpretation include:

* emotions remaining difficult to regulate after triggering events;
* emotional reactions that appear disproportionate or unusually persistent;
* recurring frustration that is difficult to resolve;
* persistent anxiety despite attempts to reduce emotional distress;
* difficulty returning to an emotionally balanced state;
* emotional responses interfering with concentration or daily functioning;
* repeatedly dwelling on emotionally distressing experiences;
* increasingly negative internal emotional dialogue;
* feeling emotionally overwhelmed by relatively ordinary challenges;
* gradual emotional depletion following repeated emotional struggles;
* emotional reactions spreading across multiple areas of life;
* difficulty shifting attention away from emotionally significant events;
* recurring patterns of emotional instability rather than isolated emotional episodes.

These themes are examples rather than required labels. Behavioural Assessment v1 or v2 may generate different behavioural descriptions that capture the same underlying pattern.

---

# 5. Caveats

Emotional Dysregulation should not be inferred merely because:

* Anxiety or Frustration temporarily increase during genuinely difficult situations;
* emotional responses remain proportionate to the circumstances;
* emotions naturally subside after appropriate recovery;
* Rumination occurs briefly following an important emotional event;
* Stress explains emotional responses without evidence of impaired regulation;
* negative Sentiment or Self-talk occur temporarily without persistent emotional instability;
* protective factors remain strong and emotional recovery is consistently observed;
* the behavioural pattern is better explained by Chronic Stress, Burnout or Depression;
* emotional reactions remain confined to isolated situations;
* there is insufficient evidence that emotional regulation itself is impaired.

The model should distinguish **strong emotional experiences from impaired emotional regulation**. Experiencing intense emotions is not, by itself, evidence of Emotional Dysregulation; the interpretation depends primarily on the user's ability to regulate, recover from and adapt to those emotional experiences.

---

# 6. Protective Factors

Protective factors do not eliminate the possibility of Emotional Dysregulation. Instead, they indicate whether the user retains sufficient psychological resources to regulate emotional experiences and recover effectively.

* **Strong Coping Ability** suggests that emotional responses are more likely to remain manageable despite temporary increases in Anxiety or Frustration.

* **High Resilience** indicates that emotionally difficult experiences are followed by meaningful recovery rather than progressively worsening instability.

* **Stable or improving Optimism** suggests that negative emotional experiences are less likely to become persistent or self-perpetuating.

* **Healthy Social Support** may provide emotional validation, perspective and practical assistance that facilitates emotional recovery.

* **Improving Anxiety and Frustration despite continuing stressors** should substantially weaken the interpretation because it indicates increasingly effective emotional regulation.

* **Behavioural evidence of quicker emotional recovery** suggests that emotional regulation mechanisms remain effective despite experiencing negative emotions.

* **Stable Sentiment and Self-talk during emotionally demanding periods** provide evidence that emotional experiences remain appropriately regulated.

* **Temporary emotional reactions should not outweigh consistent evidence of healthy emotional recovery.** The framework should recognise adaptive emotional regulation rather than assuming that the presence of negative emotions alone indicates Emotional Dysregulation.

# Depression

## 1. Purpose

Depression represents a behavioural pattern characterised by a persistent and generalized reduction in mood, psychological wellbeing and engagement with everyday life. Rather than reflecting temporary sadness or disappointment, it involves a broader alteration in emotional state, motivation, self-perception and outlook that gradually influences how the user thinks, feels and interacts with their environment.

Within this framework, Depression is interpreted as a behavioural pattern rather than a clinical diagnosis. The evaluation should therefore distinguish persistent depressive patterns from expected emotional responses to difficult life events or temporary setbacks. Particular emphasis should be placed on whether negative emotional experiences extend across multiple aspects of the user's life rather than remaining confined to a specific situation, responsibility or stressor.

---

# 2. Associated Parameters

| Sadness              | **Primary**   | Core Indicator        |           ↑          |

| Self-efficacy        | **Secondary** | Supporting Indicator  |           ↓          |
| Anxiety              | **Secondary** | Reinforcing Indicator |           ↑          |
| Rumination           | **Secondary** | Reinforcing Indicator |           ↑          |
| Emotional Exhaustion | **Secondary** | Consequence           |           ↑          |

### Review of the assignments

**Sadness** and **Sentiment Score** are the Primary Core Indicators because they capture the broad emotional shift that characterises Depression. **Motivation**, **Self-talk Score** and **Optimism** provide Primary Supporting evidence by reflecting reduced initiative, increasingly negative self-perception and diminished hopefulness, all of which contribute substantially to a depressive behavioural profile without independently defining it. **Coping Ability** is retained as a Primary Protective Factor because effective coping meaningfully reduces the likelihood that persistent emotional difficulties develop into a broader depressive pattern.

**Task Engagement** and **Emotional Exhaustion** are treated as Secondary Consequences because they commonly emerge as Depression begins influencing daily functioning. **Self-efficacy** provides Secondary Supporting evidence by reflecting declining confidence in one's ability to manage life effectively, while **Anxiety** and **Rumination** strengthen the interpretation when persistent emotional distress is accompanied by repetitive negative thinking. **Social Support** and **Resilience** act as Secondary Protective Factors that moderate concern by supporting emotional recovery and psychological adaptation. **Concentration** remains a Tertiary Consequence because cognitive slowing commonly accompanies Depression but should not substantially influence the interpretation on its own.

---

# 3. Parameter Relationships

The following examples illustrate how the model should reason about Depression rather than serving as rigid decision rules.

* **Persistent Sadness accompanied by a declining Sentiment Score** provides the strongest overall foundation for Depression, particularly when the pattern extends across multiple areas of the user's life.

* **Negative Self-talk together with declining Optimism** strengthens the interpretation by suggesting increasingly negative beliefs about oneself, the future or personal circumstances rather than temporary emotional distress.

* **Persistent Sadness accompanied by declining Motivation and Task Engagement** suggests that low mood is beginning to influence behavioural functioning rather than remaining purely emotional.

* **Increasing Rumination alongside persistent Sadness** strengthens the interpretation by suggesting that negative emotional experiences are repeatedly reinforced rather than gradually resolving.

* **Declining Self-efficacy together with reduced Motivation** suggests that the user increasingly doubts their ability to influence positive outcomes, strengthening the broader depressive profile.

* **Emotional Exhaustion developing alongside persistent low mood** may indicate that prolonged emotional difficulties are beginning to affect overall psychological functioning.

* **Anxiety accompanying persistent depressive features** should reinforce rather than define the condition. Depression and Anxiety commonly coexist but should not automatically imply one another.

* **Sadness confined to a specific event while Optimism, Motivation and Self-talk remain largely stable** should be interpreted cautiously, as it is more consistent with an appropriate emotional response than Depression.

* **Persistent negative Sentiment without meaningful Sadness or reduced Motivation** should not substantially support Depression because general emotional tone alone is insufficient.

* **Strong Coping Ability despite persistent emotional difficulties** should moderate concern by suggesting that the user retains meaningful resources for psychological recovery.

* **Improving Optimism, Self-talk and Motivation** suggests recovery even if occasional Sadness remains.

* **Depression may coexist with Burnout, Chronic Stress or Emotional Dysregulation**, but it should remain centred on a pervasive alteration in mood, self-perception and behavioural functioning rather than a single psychological domain.

* **Depressive features consistently appearing across work, academics, relationships and personal life** provide considerably stronger evidence than patterns limited to one domain.

---

# 4. Behavioural Evidence

Behavioural themes that may strengthen the interpretation include:

* persistent low mood extending beyond isolated situations;
* reduced enjoyment or interest across previously meaningful activities;
* increasingly negative views of oneself or one's future;
* persistent reduction in motivation across multiple responsibilities;
* gradual withdrawal from everyday activities;
* diminished confidence in personal abilities;
* repeatedly dwelling on negative experiences or perceived failures;
* reduced emotional responsiveness to positive experiences;
* persistent pessimism regarding future outcomes;
* difficulty initiating everyday responsibilities;
* emotional difficulties extending across multiple life domains;
* gradual decline in overall psychological wellbeing;
* persistent sense of hopelessness or emotional heaviness.

These themes are examples rather than required labels. Behavioural Assessment v1 or v2 may generate different behavioural descriptions that capture the same underlying pattern.

---

# 5. Caveats

Depression should not be inferred merely because:

* Sadness follows an isolated disappointment or loss;
* Motivation temporarily decreases during periods of fatigue or high workload;
* negative emotions remain proportionate to difficult life circumstances;
* Burnout adequately explains reduced Motivation and Engagement within a specific domain;
* Chronic Stress explains persistent pressure without broader mood changes;
* emotional reactions improve naturally following recovery or positive changes;
* Anxiety occurs without evidence of persistent low mood or negative self-perception;
* protective factors remain strong and meaningful recovery continues to occur;
* behavioural changes remain confined to one area of life;
* there is insufficient evidence that the pattern is persistent and generalized.

The model should distinguish **persistent depressive patterns from normal emotional responses**. Temporary sadness, disappointment or reduced motivation are expected aspects of human experience and should not be interpreted as Depression unless they form part of a broader and enduring behavioural pattern.

The model should also distinguish **Depression from Burnout**. Burnout primarily remains attached to sustained demands within a particular domain, whereas Depression typically influences emotional wellbeing, self-perception and engagement across multiple areas of life simultaneously.

---

# 6. Protective Factors

Protective factors do not eliminate the possibility of Depression. Instead, they indicate whether the user retains psychological resources that support recovery and reduce the likelihood of persistent depressive patterns.

* **Strong Coping Ability** suggests that the user continues responding adaptively to emotional difficulties despite experiencing periods of low mood. Persistent Sadness should still be monitored, but concern is moderated when coping consistently facilitates meaningful recovery.

* **High or improving Resilience** indicates that emotional setbacks are followed by gradual psychological recovery rather than progressively worsening emotional wellbeing.

* **Healthy Social Support** provides emotional validation, practical assistance and interpersonal connection that may substantially reduce the persistence of depressive patterns.

* **Stable or improving Optimism** suggests that the user continues perceiving positive future possibilities despite present emotional difficulties, weakening the interpretation of a pervasive depressive outlook.

* **Improving Self-talk together with increasing Self-efficacy** indicates recovery of confidence and healthier self-perception, substantially weakening the broader depressive profile.

* **Behavioural evidence of renewed Motivation, Engagement and interest in meaningful activities** should significantly reduce concern, even if occasional periods of Sadness remain.

* **Emotional improvement occurring consistently across multiple areas of life** provides stronger evidence of recovery than improvement confined to a single domain.

* **Strong Protective Factors should outweigh isolated depressive features** when they are accompanied by clear evidence of emotional recovery, adaptive functioning and restored psychological wellbeing. A temporary low mood should not outweigh a broader pattern of resilience, healthy coping and positive adaptation.

* **Recovery should be interpreted as gradual rather than instantaneous.** Improvements in Motivation, Optimism or Self-talk may appear before emotional state fully stabilises, and the framework should recognise these positive trajectories.

* **Consistent psychological adaptation despite ongoing life difficulties** should substantially weaken the interpretation of Depression, as the framework evaluates persistent behavioural patterns rather than the presence of adversity alone.

# Mental Stability

## 1. Purpose

Mental Stability represents the presence of healthy psychological functioning rather than the absence of psychological difficulty. It reflects a behavioural pattern in which the user consistently demonstrates the psychological resources required to adapt to everyday challenges, maintain emotional balance and remain meaningfully engaged with life despite experiencing ordinary stressors, setbacks or negative emotions.

Unlike the other five conditions, Mental Stability is not the absence of symptoms. It represents the presence of adaptive psychological functioning—effective coping, resilience, confidence, emotional balance and sustained engagement despite ordinary life stressors. At the same time, it reflects an internal sense of psychological equilibrium: a state in which the user experiences relative peace of mind, emotional steadiness and confidence in managing life's challenges without becoming persistently overwhelmed. It therefore serves as a positive behavioural profile rather than simply the inverse of the negative conditions.

Within this framework, Mental Stability does not imply that the user never experiences stress, anxiety, frustration or sadness. These emotions are expected aspects of everyday life and may coexist with Mental Stability when they remain proportionate, temporary and followed by healthy emotional recovery. The framework therefore values adaptability and recovery more highly than the complete absence of emotional difficulty.

The evaluation should identify persistent evidence that the user continues functioning adaptively across emotional, cognitive and behavioural domains. Rather than asking whether negative experiences are present, the framework asks whether the user consistently demonstrates the capacity to regulate emotions, recover from setbacks, maintain perspective, remain engaged with meaningful activities and preserve an overall sense of psychological wellbeing over time.

### Review of the assignments

**Coping Ability**, **Resilience**, **Self-efficacy** and **Optimism** form the Primary Core Indicators because they represent the psychological resources that enable an individual to adapt successfully to life's ordinary challenges. Rather than reflecting the absence of adversity, these parameters collectively describe the user's capacity to manage difficulties, recover from setbacks, maintain confidence and preserve a constructive outlook over time. They therefore define Mental Stability more directly than any measure of symptom reduction.

The remaining parameters primarily demonstrate how these underlying resources manifest behaviourally. **Stress**, **Anxiety** and **Emotional Exhaustion** provide Secondary Supporting evidence when they remain relatively low or recover appropriately despite ongoing demands. **Motivation**, **Task Engagement** and **Sentiment Score** indicate continued psychological investment and emotional wellbeing, while **Social Support** functions as a Secondary Protective Factor by strengthening resilience and recovery. **Sleep Quality**, **Self-talk Score** and **Concentration** provide additional evidence of healthy functioning but remain Tertiary because they refine, rather than establish, the overall interpretation.

# 3. Parameter Relationships

The following examples illustrate how the model should reason about Mental Stability rather than serving as rigid decision rules.

* **Strong Coping Ability accompanied by high Resilience and Self-efficacy** provides the strongest overall evidence for Mental Stability, indicating that the user possesses effective psychological resources for managing everyday challenges.

* **Healthy Optimism together with positive Self-efficacy** strengthens the interpretation by suggesting confidence in future outcomes and personal capability rather than unrealistic positivity.

* **Temporary increases in Stress or Anxiety followed by stable Coping Ability and Resilience** should strengthen, rather than weaken, the interpretation because successful recovery demonstrates psychological equilibrium.

* **High Motivation accompanied by sustained Task Engagement** indicates that the user continues investing meaningfully in responsibilities despite ordinary life demands.

* **Positive Sentiment Score together with constructive Self-talk** supports the interpretation that the user's emotional wellbeing and self-perception remain broadly healthy.

* **Low Emotional Exhaustion despite continuing responsibilities** suggests that recovery mechanisms remain effective and that demands are not producing sustained depletion.

* **Healthy Social Support alongside strong Core Indicators** strengthens confidence that the user possesses both internal and external resources that promote continued wellbeing.

* **Temporary setbacks followed by improving Motivation, Engagement or Sentiment** should be interpreted as evidence of adaptation rather than deterioration.

* **Brief periods of Sadness, Frustration or Stress** should not substantially weaken Mental Stability when the user consistently demonstrates emotional recovery and behavioural resilience.

* **Strong Core Indicators despite moderately elevated Stress** should generally outweigh isolated negative parameters because the framework evaluates the user's capacity to adapt rather than the absence of challenges.

* **Consistent improvement across Coping Ability, Resilience and Self-efficacy** indicates strengthening Mental Stability even if isolated emotional difficulties remain.

* **Persistent deterioration of multiple Core Indicators** should gradually weaken the interpretation, even before a specific negative condition becomes strongly supported.

* **Mental Stability may coexist with temporary features of other conditions**, provided those features remain limited, recover appropriately and do not represent persistent behavioural patterns.

* **Evidence of psychological recovery across multiple domains**—emotional, cognitive and behavioural—provides substantially stronger support than improvement confined to a single parameter.

# 4. Behavioural Evidence

Behavioural themes that may strengthen the interpretation include:

* maintaining emotional balance despite ordinary challenges;
* recovering appropriately following stressful or emotionally difficult situations;
* demonstrating confidence when approaching new or demanding responsibilities;
* remaining engaged with meaningful personal, academic or professional activities;
* adapting constructively to setbacks rather than becoming persistently discouraged;
* consistently applying healthy coping strategies during periods of stress;
* sustaining motivation despite temporary obstacles;
* maintaining realistic optimism about future outcomes;
* demonstrating balanced and constructive self-reflection;
* preserving concentration during ordinary fluctuations in workload;
* seeking support when appropriate while retaining personal autonomy;
* showing flexibility when plans or circumstances unexpectedly change;
* maintaining psychological wellbeing across multiple areas of life rather than only one;
* returning to baseline functioning after temporary emotional difficulty;
* experiencing a general sense of emotional steadiness and peace of mind.

These themes are examples rather than required labels. Behavioural Assessment v1 or v2 may generate different behavioural descriptions that capture the same underlying pattern.

---

# 5. Caveats

Mental Stability should not be inferred merely because:

* no strongly negative condition is identified;
* Stress, Anxiety or Sadness are temporarily absent;
* the user reports feeling well during a brief observation period;
* Motivation or Task Engagement are high while Core Indicators remain weak;
* positive Sentiment occurs without evidence of healthy coping or resilience;
* emotional difficulties are simply not discussed rather than genuinely absent;
* recovery has not yet been observed following meaningful challenges;
* wellbeing appears limited to one specific area of life;
* protective resources remain untested because the user has not recently encountered adversity;
* there is insufficient evidence that healthy functioning persists over time;
* the behavioural pattern is better explained by temporary circumstances than enduring psychological adaptation;
* evidence of emotional suppression or avoidance is mistaken for emotional stability.

The model should distinguish **genuine psychological wellbeing from the temporary absence of distress**. Mental Stability is established through repeated evidence of adaptive functioning, recovery and emotional balance rather than by simply observing a few negative symptoms.

The model should also recognise that **Mental Stability is compatible with ordinary human emotion**. Experiencing stress, disappointment, anxiety or sadness does not weaken the interpretation when those experiences remain proportionate, temporary and followed by healthy recovery.

---

# 6. Protective Factors

Protective factors do not merely reduce concern for negative conditions. Within Mental Stability, they actively demonstrate the psychological resources that sustain wellbeing, adaptation and long-term emotional balance.

* **Strong Coping Ability** indicates that the user consistently responds to challenges through effective behavioural and cognitive strategies, allowing emotional difficulties to be managed rather than accumulate.

* **High Resilience** suggests that setbacks are followed by meaningful recovery and continued functioning rather than progressive psychological deterioration. Recovery itself should therefore be interpreted as positive evidence rather than simply the disappearance of symptoms.

* **Stable Self-efficacy** reflects confidence in one's ability to influence outcomes and manage future challenges. This confidence supports persistence, healthy decision-making and adaptive responses during periods of uncertainty.

* **Healthy Optimism** encourages realistic hopefulness without denying present difficulties. It reduces the likelihood that temporary setbacks become generalised pessimism or psychological withdrawal.

* **Strong Social Support** provides emotional reassurance, practical assistance and perspective that reinforce existing psychological strengths. External support should complement, rather than replace, the user's internal coping resources.

* **Behavioural evidence of consistent recovery**—such as renewed Motivation, improved Engagement, healthier Self-talk or restored emotional balance—should substantially strengthen the interpretation because recovery itself is a defining characteristic of Mental Stability.

* **Sustained psychological functioning despite ordinary stressors** provides stronger evidence than functioning observed only during easy or low-demand periods. Stability is demonstrated most clearly when it remains intact under realistic life pressures.

* **Healthy functioning across emotional, cognitive and behavioural domains simultaneously** should outweigh isolated temporary difficulties, provided the broader pattern consistently reflects adaptation and equilibrium.

* **Positive trajectories should be recognised even before perfect wellbeing is achieved.** Gradual improvements in coping, resilience or emotional balance represent strengthening Mental Stability and should be valued accordingly.

* **Mental Stability should ultimately be interpreted as an enduring pattern of adaptation rather than a static emotional state.** The framework should recognise users who repeatedly regain balance after life's inevitable challenges, as this capacity for recovery and equilibrium most accurately reflects lasting psychological wellbeing.
